20 Performance Engineering

Dropping eBPF CPU Cost by About 90% with Memoization (Not AI Gen)

152 points · 29 comments · nathannaveen.dev

The authors of an open-source eBPF security agent found that most of their runtime CPU cost came from walking parent directory entries on every file-open to determine which path-based policy applies, not from enforcement itself. They added an LRU cache keyed on mount namespace, mount ID, and inode number to store the resolved policy per inode, cutting kernel CPU cost roughly 90% in their benchmark — with a safety fallback to the slow path whenever a file has more than one hard link, since a cached inode-level policy can't safely represent multiple paths.

What the comments said

  • Several pointed out that path-policy caching is a well-established pattern, comparing it to SELinux's decades-old Access Vector Cache, and suggested the article undersells its real contribution: safely computing cache keys under eBPF/kernel constraints.
  • A commenter flagged a potential edge case around a protected directory being renamed after caching; the author responded in-thread explaining the threat-model assumption and a planned cache-eviction fix.
  • Pushback that the "90% faster" headline is benchmark-specific (repeatedly opening the exact same file) and could slightly regress performance when files are never reopened.
  • A tangent question about how the path-based policy model handles bind mounts went unanswered in the thread.

24 AI & ML

Learning to solve hard problems in RL for LLMs by never giving up

103 points · 6 comments · mnoukhov.github.io

A researcher blog post describes the "Matthew Effect" in RL post-training of LLMs: across math, code, and agentic RL, reinforcement learning mostly improves performance on problems the model could already partially solve, while genuinely hard problems barely improve. The author traces this partly to GRPO-style training discarding prompts where all sampled completions are correct or all incorrect, and finds that smaller sample counts per prompt actually solve more hard problems over training than larger ones, because large sample counts waste compute rediscovering easy problems' rare wrong answers just as often as hard problems' rare right answers.

What the comments said

  • One commenter connects the "Matthew Effect" pattern to how persistently-fine-tuned internal models are believed to become highly effective over time.
  • Another flags that several early, insightful comments in the thread were auto-flagged as dead, attributing it to increasingly sophisticated AI spambots posting plausible-sounding banal commentary.

Backprop Alternative: Augmented Lagrangian Predictive Coding

122 points · 45 comments · pub.sakana.ai

Sakana AI researchers introduce PC-ALM, a biologically-motivated local learning rule that replaces backprop's synchronized forward/backward passes with per-layer dynamical systems coupled only to their neighbors. Extending standard predictive coding with per-layer Lagrange-multiplier "dual neurons," it converges to backprop's exact gradients in the linear case and successfully trains residual MLPs up to 1,000 layers deep — a regime where standard predictive coding fails — while remaining strictly layer-local, motivated by understanding how the brain might solve credit assignment without literal backprop.

What the comments said

  • Some immediate skepticism about real-world competitiveness, since reported MNIST accuracy is only around 85%, well below backprop's typical results, though others note the paper explicitly disclaims that replacing backprop is the goal.
  • Neuroscience-interested commenters connect it to Karl Friston's free-energy principle and to earlier work showing standard predictive coding approximates backprop along arbitrary computation graphs.
  • Discussion of possible upside for continual learning and energy-efficient neuromorphic implementations, tempered by notes that backprop can already support similar interleaved-inference workflows.
