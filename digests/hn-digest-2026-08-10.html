Taxi drivers rarely die of Alzheimer's

380 points · 278 comments · theconversation.com

This Conversation piece by a GIS/civil engineer explores why taxi and ambulance drivers have the lowest Alzheimer's mortality of 443 studied occupations (roughly 1 in 100 vs. 1 in 60 overall, per a 2024 study of ~9 million U.S. death certificates), while drivers on fixed routes (buses, planes) show no such benefit. It connects this to a landmark 2000 study finding London cabbies who master 'The Knowledge' develop measurably more gray matter in the posterior hippocampus, and extends the idea to a 2023 ML study showing residents of spatially complex neighborhoods have lower Alzheimer's rates, arguing that sustained real-time spatial reasoning — not driving itself — appears to exercise and protect the hippocampus.

What the comments said

  • The most common pushback is a causality/selection-effect concern: commenters suspect people prone to cognitive decline may simply stop driving taxis earlier, or that those capable of passing tests like The Knowledge already have atypical brains, rather than the job itself protecting against Alzheimer's.
  • Several questioned whether the effect still holds for modern taxi drivers who rely on GPS/turn-by-turn navigation rather than genuine internalized mental maps, since the London Knowledge-based finding may not generalize to Uber-era driving.
  • A side debate considered confounders like taxi drivers' shorter life expectancy, but a commenter noted the underlying study controlled for age and life expectancy.
  • Some found it 'strange' that a specific cognitive activity could influence a physically-rooted disease, prompting others to counter that thinking is itself a physical/neurological process and could plausibly interact with mechanisms like glymphatic clearance during sleep.
  • A tangent asked whether video games could confer similar benefits, with commenters split — some argued games lack real stakes/consequences and are too repetitive within a genre to meaningfully exercise spatial reasoning, while others allowed that varied, high-effort play might help.

Tail-call optimization in C is relatively recent (2025)

163 points · 139 comments · lwn.net

In an LWN response to an earlier piece on Python's new tail-call support, Gforth developer Anton Ertl explains that proper tail-call optimization in C is younger than many assume: the traditional caller-cleans-stack calling convention made true tail calls hard, and GCC didn't get TCO (with real limitations, including no support for indirect calls needed by interpreter dispatch) until Mark Probst's 2001 work. After reading a paper on 'Copy-and-Patch Compilation,' Ertl retested modern GCC and Clang and found indirect-call tail-call optimization now works reliably, congratulating the Python community for exploiting the technique first since Gforth hasn't adopted it yet.

What the comments said

  • Several commenters pushed back on 'relatively recent,' noting GCC has supported indirect-call TCO since around 2006 (about a fifth of GCC's lifetime, or over half if measured differently), so it isn't as new as the headline implies.
  • A recurring disagreement: is TCO merely an optimization compilers may skip, or should languages guarantee it? Multiple commenters argued for musttail-style annotations (GCC's [[gnu::musttail]]) that fail to compile rather than silently blow the stack, since unlike most optimizations TCO affects observable memory behavior.
  • Side-thread on other languages' spotty TCO support: JavaScript had TCO specified then effectively abandoned by engines, Common Lisp lacks guaranteed TCO the way Scheme has it, and MSVC reportedly didn't get reliable C tail-call optimization until sometime in the 2010s.
  • A tangent disputed the technical premise on formal grounds, noting current ISO C (C23) makes calling a function with a mismatched declaration a compile error, and that variadic functions consuming their full va_list can be validly tail-called.
  • One reply was posted seemingly by mistake, quoting an unrelated article about engagement-driven online writing and referencing a different HN thread entirely.

10 Computing History

Parametron: 50s Japanese computer that uses neither transistors nor vacuum tubes

234 points · 56 comments · ethw.org

This ETHW Milestone entry documents the parametron, a logic element invented in 1954 by University of Tokyo graduate student Eiichi Goto that used parametric oscillation in ferrite cores instead of vacuum tubes or transistors. Its low cost and electrical stability made it the basis of PC-1, Japan's first university-built stored-program computer (built with 4,200 parametrons), which became the country's fastest computer by 1958 and helped train Japan's first generation of computer engineers.

What the comments said

  • The top comment pushes back hard on the article's framing, arguing it overstates the parametron's advantages: it ran far slower than contemporary vacuum-tube machines (10-15kHz vs. MHz), its efficiency edge vanished under heavier workloads, it required bulky inductors, and it was a technological dead end that discrete transistors quickly surpassed.
  • A well-received tangent from a commenter details NEC's 1958 NEAC-1101, a 3,600-parametron machine and Japan's first computer with floating-point arithmetic, adding first-hand-style technical specs not in the article.
  • A side discussion enjoys the coincidence of inventor Eiichi Goto's name with the 'goto' statement/'considered harmful,' and notes the unrelated GotoBLAS library was named after a different Goto (Kazushige).
  • Several commenters express enthusiasm for the related Josephson-junction-based 'quantum flux parametron,' with one explaining that transistors' relentless, predictable improvement curve crowded out alternative computing technologies before they had time to mature.
  • Other asides note the parametron's appearance in the anime/manga Dr. Stone and draw a comparison to Britain's Elliott 803, which used a different but related form of magnetic-core logic.

13 Security

Exploiting System Management Mode with a very long interrupt

178 points · 76 comments · github.com

This GitHub project (from the creators of the 'sandsifter'/xoreaxeaxeax lineage of x86 exploits) demonstrates breaking System Management Mode (SMM)—the highest-privilege, normally-invisible execution mode on x86 CPUs—using nothing but an extremely long-running single instruction. SMM's security model assumes all CPU cores enter or exit SMM together, enforced by a roughly one-second synchronization timeout in firmware; by making one core execute a wide MMIO load from a deliberately slow, undocumented memory address that stalls for over a second, an attacker can keep that core outside SMM while the rest proceed, breaking the lockstep guarantee and letting the still-outside core attack the one now finishing SMM. The proof of concept is demonstrated on a Zen 3 Ryzen 7 5800H using AMD performance-counter MSRs to detect the desynchronization.

What the comments said

  • Many commenters are entertained by the project's playful presentation (the absurdly long repo name and comically long ASCII-art code block illustrating the 'long' instruction).
  • Technical discussion centers on why the SMM sync timeout exists at all; commenters explain it's there to bound worst-case wait time (e.g., for thermal emergencies) and note it's roughly a ~4 billion cycle counter.
  • A sub-thread debates whether this is really a 'long instruction' or just a slow I/O operation in disguise, likened to a socket read that's slow because the destination is far away.
  • Commenters speculate about real-world attack surface, including whether external devices (Thunderbolt, USB-tunneled PCIe, GPUs) could be used to trigger arbitrarily slow MMIO reads, generally concluding it likely still requires elevated/root-level access.
  • A clarifying sub-thread works through the exact mechanics of which core ends up attacking which, since the timing of who is 'in' vs 'out' of SMM at the moment of exploitation initially confused several readers.

224 points · 161 comments · kuber.studio

The author argues that instructing LLM agents to write in a more 'human,' casual, or simplified style (e.g. ADHD-friendly tone, Simplified Technical English) is a category error, because that stylistic compression happens during the model's actual reasoning/output process rather than after it, making it inherently lossy. The author frames today's popular 'humanizing' prompt skills as a symptom pointing at a missing architectural layer, not a genuine solution.

What the comments said

  • Several commenters share the experience of finding dense, jargon-heavy LLM prose mentally exhausting to parse and describe prompting the agent afterward to 'decompress' its own output into plainer language.
  • One commenter half-jokingly speculates that idiosyncratic LLM house-styles could function as a vendor lock-in mechanism, since agents supposedly perform best when talking to models trained in the same 'style tribe.'
  • Some pushback: a few commenters feel the article's framing is backwards, arguing skill instructions like 'ADHD mode' are actually requests for terser, more machine-like output, not humanization, and that they got the article's thesis inverted.
  • A practical counterproposal recurs across threads: just run a two-step pipeline (full answer, then a separate summarization pass) so you get both fidelity and readability, with some suggesting this should be a harness-level feature rather than a prompt hack.
