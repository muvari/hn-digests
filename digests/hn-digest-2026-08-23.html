1 Security

Malware infects Android-based automotive head unit firmware

246 points · 127 comments · securelist.com

Kaspersky researchers discovered a new multi-stage Android malware family spreading through the built-in update mechanism of DoFun automotive head unit firmware, the first documented malware infection chain specific to car head units. A legitimate analytics/update app called TWCore was abused via an MQTT-controlled flag that lets it install apps not originally present on the device, delivering a dropper, loader, and finally a clicker/proxy module. The researchers attribute the campaign with high confidence to the MoYu Group, linked to the BADBOX botnet, and say its purpose is ad fraud and building a residential proxy botnet; the vendor was notified and reportedly fixed the distribution issue.

What the comments said

  • Commenters largely agree the real value to attackers isn't the head unit itself but the always-on, clean mobile/SIM connection it provides, making it ideal residential-proxy real estate.
  • Several pointed out head units can still be a privacy risk beyond botnets, since they log location, navigation history, call logs/audio, and full contact lists when paired with a phone.
  • There was confusion clarified by other commenters between Android Auto (phone-hosted passthrough) and Android Automotive (a full separate OS built into the car, as used by Volvo), which is what's actually vulnerable here.
  • Some commenters blamed cheap aftermarket/off-brand head unit vendors and their update servers rather than major car manufacturers, while others used it as another argument against internet-connected cars in general given how long vehicles stay in service versus vendor support windows.
  • A few reacted with dark humor, noting the irony that critical infrastructure (like water-monitoring PLCs) gets ignored by attackers for years while ad-fraud-capable devices like head units are targeted almost immediately.
2 Reverse Engineering

Everything I own, owned

1,026 points · 285 comments · schlarp.com

The author used Claude Opus 5 as an agentic reverse-engineering assistant to probe the firmware of several personal peripherals — an Insta360 Link webcam, an ASUS ROG Swift monitor, and others — giving it broad goals like documenting update protocols, checking for signature validation, and finding hidden functionality. Across five devices this took about 13 hours of total AI "churn" and 98 prompts over two weeks, and the agent found weak or absent firmware protections in each case: it disabled the webcam's activity LED by patching a pattern table and reflashing over an undocumented USB command channel, and found the monitor's firmware had no real protection, letting it locate (though not yet apply) a patch to kill an unwanted "pixel cleaning" popup.

What the comments said

  • Many commenters shared their own similar wins using LLM agents to reverse-engineer obscure hardware/firmware (a GPU driver, a note-taking device's file format, a WiFi relay, an old game server), suggesting this is becoming a broadly reproducible technique rather than a one-off.
  • Some pushed back on the specific example: the monitor's 'pixel cleaning' warning isn't arbitrary bloat but likely anti-burn-in maintenance for OLED panels, and disabling it could shorten the display's life — sparking a side debate about OLED longevity and planned obsolescence versus user autonomy.
  • A recurring caution was that upcoming/existing regulations (EU RED directive, and the incoming Cyber Resilience Act) increasingly mandate signed/secure firmware updates on connected devices, which commenters expect will make this kind of DIY unlocking harder over time.
  • Reactions to the trend itself were split between excitement (that agentic RE 'democratizes' hardware hacking and erodes vendor lock-in/moats) and more measured takes noting the output is still described by the author as 'generated-slop docs' needing validation against real hardware.
  • One commenter mentioned hitting AI safety guardrails when trying similar reverse-engineering work on vintage 1980s/90s computers, contrasting with others' smooth experiences on modern peripherals.

7 Systems Engineering

How Complex Systems Fail (1998)

329 points · 72 comments · how.complexsystems.fail

A reposting of Richard Cook's 1998 essay arguing that complex systems (transportation, healthcare, power generation) are inherently hazardous and are kept safe only by layers of defenses that usually work. Catastrophe requires multiple simultaneous small failures, not a single cause, so post-accident 'root cause' attribution is a social need to assign blame rather than a technical truth. The essay also describes hindsight bias, systems running permanently in a degraded state, and operators as playing a dual role of producers and defenders against failure.

What the comments said

  • Commenters, several with direct experience in incident response and safety-critical fields, describe the essay as foundational, with one calling it a quarterly team reading and a hiring talking point.
  • There is debate over the 'root cause analysis is a fool's errand' framing: some argue root-cause language is fine if understood as 'what must we change,' while others use aviation and NASA Challenger examples to show multiple contributing causes are already standard practice, not a new insight from the essay.
  • Several commenters connect the essay's ideas to Chaos Engineering, arguing deliberate induced failure builds the resilience the essay describes, though one questions how this applies to high-stakes systems where failure can cause real harm.
  • Multiple people extend the piece's themes to Gall's Law (complex working systems evolve from simple working ones) and to broader domains like education and economics, arguing modern efficiency drives have stripped out the redundant safeguards the essay says are necessary.
  • One commenter shares a stream of real-world 'proto-accident' quotes from operators normalizing degraded conditions, illustrating the essay's point about systems running broken but functional.

13 Networking

Wi-Fi 8 is the first wireless upgrade in years that isn't chasing speed

321 points · 264 comments · xda-developers.com

Wi-Fi 8 (IEEE's "Ultra High Reliability" standard) is the first major Wi-Fi generation that does not chase higher theoretical speeds, since Wi-Fi 7 already outpaces most home internet needs. Instead its stated goals are a 25% throughput increase across varying signal-to-interference levels, 25% lower 95th-percentile latency, and 25% less packet loss, achieved through features like distributed-tone resource units, interference mitigation pilots, unequal per-stream modulation, faster channel access (P-EDCA), and seamless multi-access-point roaming. It is expected to be finalized around 2028 and is explicitly positioned as competing with future 6G cellular networks.

What the comments said

  • Many commenters agree raw speed is largely irrelevant now since ISP speeds and most home tasks don't approach current Wi-Fi limits, and welcome a shift toward latency, reliability and congestion handling.
  • Others push back, noting real use cases like local file transfers, NAS backups, and device-to-TV streaming still benefit from higher local throughput even without faster internet.
  • Skepticism runs through the thread about implementation quality, with commenters recalling that Wi-Fi 6's OFDMA and Wi-Fi 7's MLO were inconsistently or poorly implemented across vendors, and doubting Wi-Fi 8's promised gains will fully materialize in consumer hardware.
  • Real-world anecdotes describe severe interference problems (e.g., overlapping access points causing ~40% packet loss in an apartment) and enterprise/warehouse frustration with unreliable roaming, which commenters see as exactly what Wi-Fi 8's infrastructure-controlled roaming should fix.
  • Some criticize the article itself for citing theoretical "Gbit per band" throughput figures, calling that metric essentially meaningless marketing language.

The End of an Athlon

177 points · 88 comments · os2museum.com

The author explains that AMD and Intel both used exposed flip-chip PGA packaging around 2000 to improve cooling as TDPs rose past 50W, but the fragile silicon was prone to cracking during heatsink installation/removal, a risk that diminished once both companies moved to sturdier lidded and later LGA packaging.

What the comments said

  • Commenters swap first-hand technique tips for safely removing heatsinks from that era (e.g., rotating gently, doing it while the system is still warm) to avoid cracking exposed dies, treating this as a well-known hazard of the Socket A/flip-chip generation.
  • Several recall AMD chips as noticeably more brittle and prone to chipping than contemporaneous Intel Coppermine parts, speculating this came down to differences in die thickness or silicon ingot quality.
  • There's curiosity about whether the damaged chip could ever be repaired; the consensus is essentially no, short of obtaining AMD's tapeout and access to a compatible fab.
  • A side discussion clarifies CPU die structure and flip-chip/C4 packaging for commenters confused about how thin and fragile the exposed silicon actually is.
  • Several nostalgic tangents recount running old Athlon/K6-era rigs and their cooling quirks and jury-rigged fixes.
