16 Engineering History

The Helicopter with Radioactive Blades

189 points · 57 comments · hackaday.com

A Hackaday post explains IBIS (Inflight Blade Inspection System), a decades-old solution for detecting cracks in CH-53 Sea Stallion helicopter rotor blades in flight. The blades are pressurized with nitrogen; a crack lets gas leak out, which mechanically shifts a small radiation shield to expose a tiny amount of Strontium-90, letting a cockpit Geiger counter detect the failure in real time — all without any electronics, wiring, or batteries on the spinning rotor itself, a constraint that made 1960s electronics impractical.

What the comments said

  • A self-identified maintainer clarified the acronym and "BIM" pod naming, with a photo link of the actual hardware.
  • Several explained the mechanism in more detail than the article itself: gas pressure holds a spring-loaded radiation shield in place, and a leak lets it move, exposing the source.
  • Radiation-safety questions (false positives near contaminated ground, total activity involved) got detailed, reassuring answers — comparable to or less than radiation exposure from a high-altitude flight.
  • Commenters admired the "elegant, no-electronics" engineering, drawing a Chesterton's Fence comparison to solving a hard problem with mechanical simplicity rather than added complexity.

19 Programming

Replacing a Rust Enum with a 64-Bit Word Made My Interpreter 17% Faster

134 points · 54 comments · pointersgonewild.com

The developer of the Plush toy interpreter/VM explains replacing a 16-byte tagged Rust enum representing dynamic values with a custom pointer-tagging scheme packed into a single 64-bit word — exploiting 8-byte pointer alignment and the fact that 64-bit integers rarely need their full range. The change, co-designed with Claude, yielded a 17% speedup, driven less by the bit-packing cleverness itself and more by the fact that the old 128-bit enum required twice as many CPU registers per value, causing register spills that the compact representation avoids.

What the comments said

  • The author's own follow-up comment attributes much of the speedup to reduced register pressure/spills from the old 128-bit representation, not purely the bit-packing scheme — a richer explanation than the post itself gives.
  • Experienced compiler/VM engineers, including a Rust core contributor, discussed why the compiler can't and shouldn't automatically perform this kind of representation optimization, since it needs domain-specific tradeoffs the language can't infer generically.
  • The specific integer-tagging trick — using zero as the tag bits so plain add/sub instructions work directly on tagged values — got singled out as a nice, reusable idea several commenters said they'd steal.
  • Broader discussion of how this class of hand-tuned representation work has historically accounted for large chunks of the performance gap between optimized VMs like V8 and slower interpreters like CPython.
