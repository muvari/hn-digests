13 AI & ML Research

Breaking the 1.58-bit Barrier for Ternary LLMs

218 points · 34 comments · arxiv.org

Intel researchers measured the actual weight distribution of 29 real ternary LLMs and found zeros make up as much as 51.5% of weights — far from the equiprobable assumption behind the standard "1.58-bit" five-trit packing format. They introduce BITCOS, a distribution-adaptive layout (a presence bitmap plus compacted sign vector) that beats standard packing on 26 of 29 tested models, reaching 1.485 bits/weight on the sparsest model, with optimized unpacking for AVX-512, AVX2, and Intel Xe2 GPUs yielding up to 1.28x faster kernels and up to 1.27x faster end-to-end inference.

What the comments said

  • Many were delighted by the simple empirical insight — that real ternary weights are heavily zero-skewed — driving a meaningfully better compression scheme.
  • Some argued ternary quantization is the wrong approach entirely versus vector/trellis-based post-training quantization methods, though others countered that ternary's whole appeal is inference speed (every weight becomes an add/subtract/no-op), which vector quantization doesn't offer.
  • A commenter suggested squeezing further gains with arithmetic coding, with a reply noting the paper's 128-entry block size might already be a reasonable sweet spot between compression and fast random access.
  • Some speculated custom silicon built around ternary weights could be dramatically more efficient if the approach continues to pan out.

23 Programming

Anecdotally, programmers dislike "reduce"

158 points · 238 comments · evanhahn.com

The author observes that in code review, peers rarely object to map or filter but frequently flag reduce as "hard to read," and theorizes this is because map and filter guarantee something predictable about their output (same length, filtered subset), while reduce's result and behavior are much less constrained — especially in imperative languages where it's easy to smuggle in side effects, making it feel like a for-loop with worse syntax.

What the comments said

  • Many agreed with the core theory: map/filter tell you something about their output shape up front, while reduce could produce (or do) almost anything, adding cognitive load.
  • A side debate covered reduce's competing names across languages and paradigms (fold, accumulate), with some arguing this fragmentation itself adds friction versus a single, universally recognized name.
  • Defenders gave concrete cases where reduce is genuinely the cleanest option, like folding a list of Spark dataframes into one with a single union call.
  • Several noted a common reduce foot-gun: forgetting the correct identity/start value (0 for sum, 1 for product) leads to silent bugs.
  • In Python specifically, multiple commenters argued built-ins like sum, min, max, any, and all cover the vast majority of cases that would otherwise reach for reduce.
