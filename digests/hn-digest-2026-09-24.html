3 Privacy & Encryption

Two-tier encryption in the UK

472 points · 421 comments · macanorak.com

The article traces the history from Apple's public 2016 fight with the FBI over the San Bernardino iPhone through to the UK's secret January 2025 Technical Capability Notice ordering Apple to build a way into end-to-end encrypted iCloud data worldwide. It explains the legal/technical distinction between iCloud's default encryption (which Apple can decrypt on request) and Advanced Data Protection's true end-to-end encryption, and describes how Apple responded not by building a backdoor but by withdrawing ADP for new UK users in February 2025 — creating a "two-tier" system where only UK users who enabled it before that date still have it.

What the comments said

  • Some dismiss it as a niche concern most UK citizens won't notice or remember amid bigger worries like cost of living and infrastructure.
  • Others see the secret TCN-and-gag-order regime as effectively outlawing E2EE by fiat, comparing it to other "not illegal, but you can't build a business around it" style regulatory backdoors.
  • A practical subthread debates whether switching an Apple ID's region to enable ADP and then reverting to the UK actually works, with mixed reports and caveats about Family Sharing/subscriptions.
  • A skeptical commenter argues the Apple-vs-FBI "standoff" is overstated, claiming Apple routinely hands over non-E2EE iCloud data via legal orders and that the backdoor fight was largely theater; this drew pushback questioning why the FBI would then make such a public case of it.
  • Side discussion covers whether the UK could realistically target open-source E2EE tools like SimpleX or GrapheneOS, given they can be freely forked.

Show HN: Make cursed fonts like Times New Bastard

751 points · 101 comments · bastardica.mitpit.com

Bastardica is a browser-based tool (running locally via Pyodide and fontTools, with no uploads) for creating "bastard" fonts in the style of Times New Bastard, by mixing glyphs from a base font with one or more "mix-in" fonts at a configurable stride, offset, and scale. Fearless SIMD v1.0

276 points · 42 comments · linebender.org

Linebender announced version 1.0 of fearless_simd, a Rust crate providing SIMD abstractions with no ad-hoc unsafe code, alongside a new fearless_simd_macros crate adding a #[simd] attribute for ergonomic function multiversioning. The design relies on a kernel! macro built on target_feature v1.1 for safe intrinsics access, plus a small safe-transmute module handling load/store operations, so only two self-contained building blocks need to be audited for memory safety across the whole codebase. 17 Networking

Making Tailscale Faster

242 points · 102 comments · tailscale.com

Tailscale details several performance improvements: on Linux and Android, it now avoids copying every small packet into an oversized 64KiB buffer (worth roughly a 5% speedup) and has shortened packet queues to cut memory overhead and latency, freeing capacity for a new multi-queue architecture. That architecture gives subnet routers, app connectors, and exit nodes parallel per-stream lanes across CPU cores instead of one shared ordered pipeline, boosting throughput especially for connector and exit-node workloads with many short-lived connections. The post also previews writev-based zero-copy writes to the kernel and upcoming netmap caching to speed up client startup, with the multi-queue work landing in the second half of 2026.

What the comments said

  • Tailscale's own cofounder (apenwarr) joins the thread to explain that wireguard-go optimizations previously beat kernel WireGuard's speed, and argues kernel mode isn't a silver bullet — DPDK-style userspace is the real path to very high throughput, partly because WireGuard's crypto isn't hardware-accelerated.
  • A recurring complaint is that Tailscale's userspace WireGuard-on-Linux still can't hit the throughput of kernel WireGuard or IPsec, with a 6-year-old open GitHub issue cited as evidence of neglect.
  • Some commenters say they abandoned Tailscale for plain self-hosted WireGuard plus dynamic DNS, finding it simpler and faster, while others push back that Tailscale's zero-config NAT traversal and no-port-forwarding setup are worth the tradeoff.
  • A side debate covers post-quantum WireGuard, with critics arguing that PQ key sizes would push WireGuard toward IPsec/IKE-level complexity, undermining its simplicity advantage.
  • Scattered real-world gripes include latency degrading sharply around 250 concurrent sessions and a wish for lower battery drain when running as an exit node.
18 Space

Google's Project Suncatcher to put ML infrastructure in space

202 points · 413 comments · blog.google

Google outlines Project Suncatcher, a research moonshot testing whether space could eventually host scalable ML infrastructure, via a prototype satellite launching on SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare (with partner Planet) to see how its Trillium TPUs handle real spaceflight conditions. Ground testing already showed the hardware survived simulated launch vibration and g-forces, and TPUs exposed to proton radiation at UC Davis's Crocker Nuclear Laboratory withstood more than five years' worth of expected mission dose. The unresolved hard problem is cooling: with no airflow in vacuum, heat can only be shed via radiators, and Google is testing heat-pipe-and-radiator combinations, with a longer-term vision of linking satellite clusters via high-bandwidth optical links for larger orbital AI workloads by 2027.

What the comments said

  • A lot of skepticism centers on the physics: commenters run back-of-envelope radiator math (Stefan-Boltzmann) showing even a modest 1MW of compute would need thousands of square meters of solar panels and radiators, implying genuine "orbital megastructures" at any meaningful scale.
  • Some dismiss the project as more marketing/PR than a serious engineering path, or speculate it's connected to Alphabet's financial stake in SpaceX rather than a standalone bet on space compute.
  • Others find the formation-flying requirement — dozens of satellites packed within 100–200m of each other while moving at orbital velocity, needed for optical-link bandwidth — even more striking than the cooling challenge.
  • A recurring cynical line: it may end up cheaper to launch a data center into orbit than to get one through local permitting and zoning on Earth.
  • A few compare the announcement to SpaceX's trajectory of shifting the Overton window on "impossible" ideas, while others remain doubtful space-based compute will ever beat ground-based economics.
