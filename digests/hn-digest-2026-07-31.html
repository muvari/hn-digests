1 Engineering

Elevators

1,292 points · 314 comments · john.fun

An interactive explainer walks through how elevator dispatch algorithms actually work, from the simple SCAN/LOOK sweep patterns to Otis's RSR (Relative System Response) scoring system that re-optimizes car assignments every five seconds. Using live simulations, the author shows that "smarter" destination-dispatch kiosks, where you pick your floor before boarding, generally produce worse wait times than plain up/down buttons, because locking a rider to one car up front sacrifices the flexibility to re-route as conditions change. It also finds that RSR only beats basic LOOK scheduling in certain traffic and building-size regimes; under heavy load or in small buildings, simpler LOOK can win.

What the comments said

  • Many commenters pointed to Elevator Saga, a browser game where you program elevator schedulers, as essential companion reading and a surprisingly deep rabbit hole.
  • Engineers who built third-party elevator integrations described how frenetically the cars are actually scheduled behind the scenes, even when waiting feels interminable from the lobby.
  • Several drew parallels to disk-scheduling algorithms, noting SCAN and LOOK originated as, and share names with, classic disk head-seek strategies.
  • One commenter shared a painful 2011 university assignment on agent-based elevator control, prompting others to reminisce about how deceptively hard the problem is.
  • A few offered improvement ideas, like redistributing idle cars that end up stopped too close together, and joked about whether elevator programmers are secretly sadists.

The session you cannot take with you

751 points · 213 comments · earendil.com

Earendil Engineering argues that modern inference APIs, including OpenAI's Responses API and Google's new Gemini Interactions API, are increasingly returning "provider-sealed state" rather than portable transcripts: encrypted reasoning blobs, hidden tool and search results, and server-stored response IDs that only the original provider can decrypt or resolve. The piece proposes five concrete tests for real session ownership, inspection, export, replay, audit, and deletion, and argues that features marketed as privacy protections, like encrypted_content, mostly shield data from the user rather than from the provider, cementing lock-in behind a thin veneer of user benefit.

What the comments said

  • Some pushed back, wondering whether the argument is self-serving for an agent-harness vendor, and pointed to a legitimate security rationale, preventing role-confusion or prompt-injection style attacks, for hiding raw reasoning traces; others rebutted that hiding chain-of-thought doesn't meaningfully stop such attacks.
  • A technical sub-thread explained why full conversation history must still be resent despite hidden reasoning: KV-cache economics require providers to keep the full context to keep caching cheap, and encrypted reasoning blobs must round-trip to the same provider to stay usable.
  • Readers connected the piece to broader platform lock-in patterns, invoking comparisons to "Sign in with Google" and the IndieWeb POSSE philosophy, and expressed intent to avoid depending on a single AI ecosystem.
  • Several commenters thanked the named authors directly for the writeup, while one asked what it would take for the ideas to become more than "just blog fodder."

189 points · 95 comments · jeffgeerling.com

Jeff Geerling documents upgrading his Mac Studio from built-in 10GbE to 25GbE using a cheap ex-server OCP 2 NIC paired with a Thunderbolt 3 adapter board, since Macs can't accept PCIe cards directly and official 25G Thunderbolt adapters cost $400–$1,100. 13 Science

The most official water costs $120k a gallon

186 points · 148 comments · signoregalilei.com

The post explains that even chemically pure water isn't uniform, because isotopic variation in hydrogen and oxygen subtly shifts its freezing point. To standardize precision measurement, scientists adopted "Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water" (VSMOW) in 1961, an internationally agreed reference sample distilled from Pacific Ocean water, against which all triple-point thermometer calibrations trace their lineage. Because supply is limited, a 5ml ampoule costs $159 — roughly $120,000 per gallon — and this standard underpinned the official SI definition of the Kelvin scale until 2019.

What the comments said

  • Several commenters compared it to other absurdly priced reference materials, like NIST's certified peanut-butter standard, noting that what you're really paying for is the certified data sheet, not the substance itself.
  • A side debate broke out over Celsius vs. Kelvin as the "better" temperature scale, with disagreement over whether Kelvin truly avoids arbitrary reference points.
  • Commenters did napkin math on pricing other isotope-heavy water, estimating costs for deuterium water and pure tritium ("super-heavy") water, with one estimate near $44 million per gallon.
  • Some pushed back on the "expensive" framing, pointing out that specialty lab materials like pure xenon cost more by mass, though others noted people find rare elements intuitively expensive in a way rare water isn't.
