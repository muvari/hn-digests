Samsung is expected to more than double output of its HBM4 and HBM4E DRAM

518 points · 376 comments · en.sedaily.com

Samsung is projected to more than double output of its HBM4 and HBM4E high-bandwidth memory next year, driven by a planned 2.5x increase in glass-carrier cleaning capacity, a key material for thinning HBM wafers. Sixth-gen HBM4 mass shipments began in February on a 10nm-class process, and 12-layer HBM4E samples went to customers including Nvidia in May. Total HBM wafer input is expected to grow roughly 40% next year, with the HBM4 family rising from about 40% to 80% of shipment mix as HBM4E ramps.

What the comments said

  • Commenters debated whether more HBM supply will relieve consumer DRAM prices, with several arguing HBM and consumer DRAM aren't interchangeable across fabs.
  • A recurring frustration: regular DDR4/DDR5 prices have roughly tripled in a couple of years, which many blame on AI demand, though others called it ordinary supply-and-demand rather than "abuse."
  • Some explained why consumer devices don't just use HBM instead of DIMMs — it's tied directly to the processor package (as in Apple Silicon), trading upgradeability for bandwidth, at roughly 3x the fab capacity per byte.
  • A few pushed back on reading this as an AI-bubble signal, noting HBM is purpose-built datacenter memory rather than a broad economic indicator.

A Necessary History of the Oddest Letter: W

179 points · 91 comments · lithub.com

An excerpt from Danny Bate's book on the alphabet's history traces how the letter W emerged in the 6th century, when post-Roman Latin scribes needed a way to spell Germanic /w/ sounds that classical Latin no longer had a letter for (Latin's V had shifted to /v/). Scribes began doubling the letter U — "UU" — which fused into "W" by around the 11th century, making English's /w/ sound a rare survivor of a consonant most other Indo-European languages lost or altered.

What the comments said

  • A popular thread mocked W as the only English letter needing three syllables to say, spawning joke campaigns to rename it something shorter like "woo" or "vay" (as in German).
  • Commenters compared W's fate across languages — German pronounces it /v/, and Finnish historically used it interchangeably with U/V until 19th–20th century spelling reforms, explained in detail by a few linguistically minded users.
  • Several drew a parallel to "thorn" (þ) and "wynn," other lost English letters, and the "Ye Olde Shoppe" misconception stemming from thorn being replaced by Y in print.
  • One commenter admitted misreading the title as "oldest" rather than "oddest" and read the whole piece confused as a result — a comment others found relatable.

UTF-8000: Unlimited UTF-8

130 points · 115 comments · utf-8000.jb2170.com

UTF-8000 is a satirical-but-rigorously-specified extension to UTF-8 that removes the codepoint ceiling by allowing multiple consecutive "start bytes" at the beginning of a code unit, letting encoded values grow arbitrarily large while preserving UTF-8's core self-synchronization property — you can always tell from a single byte's top bits whether it starts a new character or continues one.

What the comments said

  • Commenters had fun speculating this could eventually be (ab)used for an unbounded emoji range, alien languages, or an explosion of Unicode's already-controversial scope.
  • A well-sourced thread explained why standard UTF-8/Unicode caps out at 21 bits (mandated for UTF-16 compatibility) despite the original 1990s spec supporting up to 31 bits, with links to the relevant RFCs.
  • Someone flagged that regular UTF-8's invalid 0xFF-byte detection trick doesn't carry over to UTF-8000, though others noted that's a minor loss since it isn't a reliable validity check anyway.
  • A widely-shared reaction quoted Ken Thompson dismissing the idea as pointless — "like replacing IPv6 with IPv50" — though others defended it as an elegant, minimal extension of UTF-8's original design.
