The computer that helped win World War II

203 points · 78 comments · spectrum.ieee.org

The IEEE Spectrum piece recounts how British engineer Tommy Flowers built Colossus, the world's first large-scale programmable electronic digital computer, at Bletchley Park to break the German Lorenz cipher ('Tunny'), a more advanced encryption system than Enigma. What the comments said

  • A Bletchley Park visitor recounted that most Colossus machines were destroyed after the war as too sensitive, later rebuilt from memory by retired engineers as a hobby project; others corrected this, noting some machines were actually moved to what became GCHQ, and a working replica exists today at The National Museum of Computing.
  • Several threads covered Enigma's design flaws (its reflector meant a letter could never encode as itself) and German operator errors like repetitive message formats, which commenters said were exploitable weaknesses that better discipline or design could have avoided.

19 Computer Graphics

Ray tracing massive amounts of animated geometry using tetrahedral cages

106 points · 13 comments · gpuopen.com

AMD researchers describe a technique (award-winning at High-Performance Graphics 2026) for ray tracing massive amounts of animated geometry by wrapping meshes in a coarse tetrahedral 'cage.' Instead of animating and rebuilding acceleration structures for every triangle each frame, only the low-resolution cage is animated at runtime, while rays entering a tetrahedron are transformed into a static rest-pose space to intersect pre-built, reusable mini-BLASes. The approach approximates motion as piecewise-linear deformation and is best suited to swaying vegetation, grass, crowds, or distant characters rather than sharp character deformation, demonstrated at roughly 585 million animated triangles at 60fps on an AMD RX 9070 XT.

What the comments said

  • The most substantive discussion was commenters explaining, for people unfamiliar with ray tracing internals, why animation is so much costlier than in rasterization: ray tracing needs a spatial acceleration structure (BVH) that must be rebuilt or repaired whenever vertex positions change, whereas rasterization just processes a flat, ordered triangle list each frame.
  • One commenter framed it as ray tracing being fundamentally a global visibility problem versus rasterization's more localized one, adding that cache coherency during ray traversal ('primary rays cache, secondary trash') compounds the cost.
  • Reception was positive but light on debate, with a couple of commenters just praising the technique and sharing a link to the conference presentation video for those wanting to see it in motion.
  • No dissent or pushback on the paper's claims appeared in the thread; the comments were mostly explanatory/educational rather than critical.
