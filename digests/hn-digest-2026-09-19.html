3 Neuroscience

Two parallel neural ectoderm progenitors contribute to the developing brain

631 points · 247 comments · med.stanford.edu

Stanford researchers report that the human forebrain/midbrain and hindbrain arise from two entirely separate, mutually exclusive progenitor cell populations from the earliest stages of embryonic development, rather than from one shared progenitor as long assumed. The two lineages — marked by the genes Otx2 and Gbx2 respectively — also carry fundamentally different chromatin configurations that lock in their fates, explaining decades of failed attempts to coax one progenitor type into becoming the other. The discovery let the team grow functional human hindbrain motor neurons in a dish for the first time, opening research avenues for brainstem diseases like ALS and spinal muscular atrophy.

What the comments said

  • Several commenters pushed back on the "two separate organs" framing as overhyped, noting this is really about distinct progenitor cell populations, not literally two organs.
  • Others drew parallels to older ideas like hemispheric lateralization and the "triune brain," noting layered-brain concepts aren't new — what's new is identifying the distinct progenitor genes.
  • Commenters linked the finding to a related front-page story on ctenophores, wondering if nervous systems evolved independently more than once.
  • Some found it striking that forebrain and hindbrain trace to mutually exclusive lineages from the earliest stages of development.
  • A pointer to the underlying bioRxiv preprint (from 2025) noted the result had already been circulating before formal publication.

5 Mathematics

If math is more than proof, we need to better celebrate the rest of it

365 points · 269 comments · terrytao.wordpress.com

In this guest post on Terence Tao's blog, Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown) argues that as AI increasingly automates proof-generation, mathematics needs to formally recognize a second kind of contribution: the "motivated explanation," which clarifies not just that a theorem is true but why it's the right question to ask and where the ideas come from. Unlike proofs, motivated explanations start from an intuitive (even wrong) idea and build toward the definitions rather than starting from them, and their validity is inherently more subjective — but Sanderson argues that squishiness shouldn't disqualify them from academic credit, pointing to works like the Princeton Companion to Mathematics as proof this kind of writing already has real value.

What the comments said

  • Commenters connected the piece to Goodhart's Law — once proof-generation becomes the target/metric, it stops indicating real understanding.
  • Some worried this retreat toward "motivated explanation" and intuition is a rationalization as AI encroaches on formal proof, comparing it to historical retreats into subjective territory when a field's old certainties erode.
  • Several pointed out the post is a guest piece by Grant Sanderson, not Terence Tao himself, despite being hosted on Tao's blog.
  • A few cited the historical Poincaré–Hilbert debate over intuition versus rigor as a precedent for the current tension.
  • Others weren't convinced this addresses the underlying economic question — even if AI lacks "taste," cost and capability, not philosophy, will determine who keeps getting paid to do mathematics.

24 Legal History

Deodands put a price on objects that caused death

90 points · 35 comments · daily.jstor.org

This JSTOR Daily piece explains the deodand, an English legal doctrine dating back roughly eight centuries under which any moveable object that directly caused a person's death was forfeited to the crown — in theory for charitable use, in practice often given to victims' families. The law's scope was famously inconsistent, and it was ultimately abolished in 1846 specifically because early railway accidents made juries assess deodands at ruinous costs against railway companies. The abolition, intended as reform, ironically left families of railway accident victims with no legal right to any compensation at all.

What the comments said

  • Commenters added legal detail: deodand-derived civil asset forfeiture is a documented legacy in American law.
