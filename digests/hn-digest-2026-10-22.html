1 Security

eMudhra to exit global SSL/TLS certificate business

1,096 points · 702 comments · emudhra.com

eMudhra, an Indian certificate authority, announced it is voluntarily exiting the global SSL/TLS certificate business following a recent security incident involving its CAs. The company says it revoked the affected intermediate CAs within 14 hours, that no end-entity certificates were abused and no data was breached, and that SSL/TLS is only about 1% of its revenue — so it will refocus on its core PKI, eSignature, and identity businesses. It plans a "seamless" transition for existing customers via free replacement certificates and says it will complete its remaining obligations to browser root programs, including a full root-cause report.

What the comments said

  • Many commenters doubt that "withdrawing" actually repairs eMudhra's reputation, joking it will simply rebrand and quietly re-enter the CA business later.
  • Others point to a reportedly scathing list of questions the Chrome/Mozilla root programs sent eMudhra as evidence of how serious the underlying failure was.
  • Some blame the CA/Browser Forum and root programs themselves for ever trusting a company with this track record in the first place.
  • A side thread speculates about the geopolitical optics of an India-based CA being singled out, though others argue the failure is structural and could have happened to any CA anywhere.
2 Policy

Quad9 to exit France

1,265 points · 425 comments · quad9.net

Quad9, the Swiss nonprofit DNS resolver, announced it will stop serving France on November 24 rather than comply with a French court order — won by broadcaster beIN — requiring it to block DNS resolution for certain domains. The court reduced the daily fine from €580,000 to €1,000, but Quad9 says compliance is technically impossible since it keeps no records tying users to jurisdictions, and argues that complying would set a precedent letting any country's courts dictate global DNS blocking. It stresses it doesn't condone piracy but believes resolvers are the wrong target for fighting it.

What the comments said

  • Many compare punishing a recursive resolver to punishing a phone book for listing a criminal's number, and see Quad9 as an easy target precisely because it lacks Google- or Cloudflare-scale resources to fight back.
  • Commenters note this is really driven by beIN's sports-broadcasting rights, not French privacy regulators, and some are annoyed by off-topic tangents conflating this with unrelated cases.
  • A sub-thread digs into why "recursive" resolution is legally significant, concluding nearly all public resolvers are inherently recursive, so the framing may be an artifact of how the lawsuit was argued.
  • Some draw a cautionary parallel to other cases where blocking measures were deemed compliant yet a plaintiff circumventing them via VPN still led to contempt-of-court fines.

562 points · 174 comments · simedw.com

Using LLM for confidence scoring is a bad idea

210 points · 100 comments · rumple.github.io

The post argues that LLM-generated "confidence scores" (e.g., "I'm 85% sure") are meaningless because models produce such numbers the same way they produce any other text — as a plausible continuation, not a calibrated statistical estimate grounded in evidence and checked against real outcomes. Unlike a weather forecaster's calibrated probability, an LLM's stated confidence shifts when you merely reword the question, because it was never trained or scored against whether its stated confidence actually matches its accuracy.

What the comments said

  • Many agree with the core framing, reinforcing that LLMs are "next-token predictors" dressed in the language of reasoning, with a side debate over whether that description is itself reductive.
  • Some argue you could, in principle, RL-train a model specifically against real calibration targets to get genuinely meaningful confidence scores, rather than assuming it's permanently impossible.
  • A commenter with a statistics background reflects on how modern ML practice spends far less time on rigorous output interpretation than classical statistics did.
  • A tangential subthread mocks LLMs' tendency toward "average," unoriginal taste when used for subjective rating or scoring tasks.
