719 points · 433 comments · krebsonsecurity.com

KrebsOnSecurity covers a Bitsight investigation into H96-brand Android TV streaming sticks that run a dual-purpose fraud scheme built by China's Zhejiang Fengwo IoT Technology. When a TV is on and streaming, the device quietly relays residential proxy traffic; when the TV is off, it switches to clicking fake ads on AI-generated websites while spoofing itself as various Samsung, Vivo, Huawei, and Xiaomi phone models to advertisers. What the comments said

  • Commenters distinguished deliberate factory-installed malice (this case) from mere incompetence — an unpatched, unmaintained Android box left open to being hijacked into the same kind of botnet — noting the end harm to the owner is similar either way.
  • Opinions split on the ethics of the hidden proxy side of the scheme: some argued running a proxy isn't inherently bad (comparing it to Tor exit nodes) and object mainly to it happening covertly, while others said stealing bandwidth and defrauding ad networks without consent is fraud regardless.
  • Several reactions blamed the "too good to be true" business model more than buyers, though others (with anecdotes about relatives falling for near-identical too-good deals) argued consumers share some responsibility for ignoring obvious red flags.
  • Someone highlighted that this isn't an isolated device line — a proxy-tracking service, Synthient, tracks almost 1,000 different streaming box, digital picture frame, and IoT models known to bundle this kind of hidden proxy or fraud software.

8 AI & ML

The Economic Benefit of Refactoring

241 points · 101 comments · martinfowler.com

A Thoughtworks CTO describes building a roughly 150,000-line Rust/TypeScript application almost entirely with coding agents (mostly Claude Code) without reading most of the generated code, watching one file balloon to over 17,000 unrefactored lines in the data-access layer. He then ran a controlled experiment: after each step of a disciplined refactor, he had a fresh agent with no memory of prior steps perform the same representative change and measured token cost, finding that input tokens stayed flat through most of the refactor before dropping sharply once the largest single file was finally broken up — showing refactoring measurably lowers the token cost of future agent-driven changes.

What the comments said

  • Commenters found the empirical framing refreshing — quantifying refactoring's value in token cost rather than hand-wavy productivity claims — though several noted the dollar savings shown (about 40 cents per change) are trivial next to a human engineer's hourly cost of guiding the refactor.
  • A widely-quoted finding was that Claude built the sprawling, un-refactored file without pushback and needed active human direction to identify what and how to refactor, reinforcing that agents don't self-correct toward better structure unprompted.
  • Several were surprised total lines of code barely changed (one 17K-line file became 19 files of similar combined size), sparking debate over whether LOC is even a meaningful proxy for code quality.
  • Multiple commenters agreed the underlying lesson — LLMs benefit greatly from well-factored code but aren't good at producing it themselves — mirrors long-standing human engineering experience, and stressed solid test coverage is what actually protects against regressions, AI-driven or not.
