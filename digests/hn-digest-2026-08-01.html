1 AI & Data Viz

Flint: A Visualization Language for the AI Era

262 points · 67 comments · microsoft.github.io

Flint is a Microsoft Research “intermediate visualization language” that lets AI agents (or humans) describe a chart with a compact, semantic specification and compiles that single spec into native output for Vega-Lite, ECharts, Chart.js, Plotly, or Excel. It uses 70+ semantic field types (Rank, Temperature, Price, Country, etc.) so the compiler can automatically pick sensible scales, layouts, and labels instead of requiring verbose manual configuration. The pitch is that agents can reliably produce polished charts without deep charting expertise, and a bundled MCP server lets agents pick templates and validate specs.

What the comments said

  • Several commenters were unconvinced the “AI era” framing means much, since an LLM can already write a one-off Plotly or D3 chart from scratch in seconds.
  • Skeptics questioned the need for yet another JSON chart spec when Vega-Lite — one of Flint's own backends — already solves this, and compared the pattern to the endless cycle of charting libraries reinventing each other.
  • Others pushed back that having an LLM read and maintain a chart later is different from having it generate one once, which is where a structured intermediate format helps.
  • A tangent into ggplot2 and Wilkinson's “Grammar of Graphics” praised older, hand-drafted statistical charts for having more character than modern default chart styling.
  • One commenter linked to a larger discussion of the same project from 22 days earlier.
2 Business

How Google helped destroy adoption of RSS feeds (2023)

525 points · 182 comments · openrss.org

This 2023 piece argues Google has systematically undermined RSS adoption through an embrace-extend-extinguish pattern: it built products around the open RSS protocol to gain trust and market share, then quietly dropped support once users were locked in. The author traces this from the removal of Chrome's built-in RSS discovery button to the shutdown of Google Reader, framing each move as protecting Google's ad-driven, walled-garden interests at the expense of the open web. The piece contends this pattern did lasting damage to RSS's mainstream adoption even though the protocol itself remains alive among enthusiasts.

What the comments said

  • Reaction was strongly emotional, with many commenters describing Google's handling of Reader and RSS as a formative, still-resented betrayal.
  • Debate over motive: some argued RSS simply couldn't carry ads as easily as a feed you control, others said it was really about pushing the doomed Google+ rather than ad revenue directly.
  • Several tied Reader's 2013 shutdown to a broader sense that this was the moment the “open web” began losing ground to closed social platforms.
  • Commenters noted RSS still has real inertia today — WordPress generates feeds by default, and tools like Nitter or Bluesky's native feeds let even social platforms be consumed the old way.
  • A few pointed out Google's reputation as an employer has soured in tandem, with “I worked at Google” now read more ambivalently than it once was.

7 Systems & Debugging

RipGrep musl binaries occasionally segfault during very-large searches

272 points · 182 comments · github.com

A ripgrep GitHub issue reports that musl-linked (x86_64-unknown-linux-musl) release binaries occasionally segfault during very large searches, first noticed via the ripgrep bundled with OpenAI Codex. A linked independent analysis traces the crash to a Linux kernel bug involving stale page-table entries and a missing TLB flush, rather than a bug in ripgrep or musl itself, reduced to a small, reproducible case.

What the comments said

  • Commenters were impressed by the kernel-level sleuthing in the linked writeup, though some pushed back hard on parts of its explanation, arguing an extra TLB flush being “wrong” misunderstands how CPUs are allowed to behave.
  • Multiple people asked why this reproduces specifically with musl; the leading theory is that musl's allocator hands out freshly faulted pages directly to the app (unlike glibc/jemalloc, which pre-allocate in batches), narrowing the race window that triggers the kernel bug.
  • It was noted ripgrep already switches to jemalloc as its allocator on 64-bit musl builds specifically because musl's own allocator performs poorly under contention, which several found ironic given this bug.
  • A side discussion argued that the deeper lesson is architectural: programs that avoid frequent allocation (by reusing buffers) sidestep allocator-related bugs like this one almost by construction.
  • One-word reactions (“everything is broken lol”) captured a general sense of resigned amusement at a kernel-level bug surfacing through something as ordinary as ripgrep.

14 AI & ML

Explorative modeling: Train on the best of K guesses

102 points · 25 comments · alexiglad.github.io

A research blog post (with accompanying paper and code) introduces “Explorative Modeling” (XM), proposed as a third axis for pretraining generative models alongside data and parameter scale. The core idea addresses a known failure mode: when there are many valid outputs for a prompt (e.g. countless valid images of “a dog”), directly regressing to predict one output causes the model to learn the blurry average of all valid answers instead of a sharp, realistic sample. By training on the best of K sampled guesses, the authors report gains that grow with scale — up to 6.2x sample efficiency, 4.1x FLOP efficiency, and matching diffusion models on control tasks with far less inference compute.

What the comments said

  • Enthusiastic commenters called it potentially important if the reported numbers hold up at larger scale, with one saying every image model trained before this approach could be rendered obsolete.
  • A more technical reply situated the work within older “winner-take-all” and K-modal generative modeling literature, crediting it as a nice modern integration into diffusion/flow pipelines while flagging a real cost: K-1 extra forward passes during training.
  • Someone questioned why the piece doesn't discuss GANs, which historically produced sharp, non-averaged samples via an adversarial discriminator rather than direct regression.
  • Multiple commenters admitted they lacked the background to judge the claims' significance but found the writeup compelling and well explained.
  • Direct links to the project page, arXiv paper, and code were shared for readers who wanted to dig into the details themselves.

19 Formal Verification

Postmortem for Kernel Soundness Bug #14576

146 points · 55 comments · leodemoura.github.io

Lean creator Leonardo de Moura's postmortem details soundness bug #14576: on July 25, an AI-assisted, sorry-free “disproof” of the Collatz conjecture was published, which turned out to exploit a genuine kernel bug rather than prove anything about Collatz itself. The bug involved nested inductive types with “phantom” parameters that could escape type-checking, letting the kernel be tricked into accepting a proof of False; it was reported on July 28 and patched within about an hour. A second, unrelated bug in nanoda, an independent external Lean checker that should have caught the fake proof, had separately been found and fixed about a week earlier.

What the comments said

  • Multiple commenters drew the core lesson explicitly: a formally verified proof is only as trustworthy as its verifier, and this episode isn't really specific to LLM-generated proofs — any code exploiting a kernel bug would have the same effect.
  • Some found reassurance in the fact that catching this required two independent, unrelated bugs in two different checkers to both be present simultaneously, though others noted this still means redundancy alone isn't a guarantee without keeping both checkers current.
  • Clarifying replies emphasized this was an implementation bug in the kernel's handling of a specific type-theory feature, not a hole in Lean's underlying mathematical foundations.
  • Someone asked why a Collatz counterexample wasn't trivially easy to verify; replies explained the claimed counterexample was of a form (“diverges to infinity”) that can't be checked in finite time, unlike a simple cycling counterexample.
  • A comparison to a similar historical soundness bug in the Metamath proof system reinforced the view that these bugs are simply a recurring hazard of complex verifier implementations, not unique to Lean.
