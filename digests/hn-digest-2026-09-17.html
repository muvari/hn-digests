1 Programming

Nvidia announces native GPU programming in Rust

946 points · 395 comments · developer.nvidia.com

NVIDIA introduced two ways to write GPU kernels natively in Rust rather than wrapping other languages: cuda-oxide, a custom rustc codegen backend for the SIMT model that compiles through Pliron IR and LLVM to PTX (requires nightly Rust and Linux), and cutile-rs, a Tile-based model that runs on stable Rust 1.89+ with CUDA 13.3 and no custom LLVM. Both enforce memory safety at compile time (via DisjointSlice/launch contracts or tensor ownership), and cutile-rs is already used in production projects like HuggingFace's inference engines. NVIDIA says it plans interoperability between CUDA Rust, C++, and Python so choosing Rust won't lock developers out of the rest of the ecosystem.

What the comments said

  • Rust CUDA users compared the new tools to existing options like cudarc, noting cuda-oxide trades a familiar, example-rich standard CUDA dialect for a new Rust-native kernel dialect with less tooling friction but a steeper "trust the new thing" cost.
  • Several were skeptical the language choice matters much for kernels themselves, pointing to Triton-style DSLs as the more relevant alternative for higher-level abstraction, and questioning what concrete benefit "Rust kernels" bring end users.
  • A recurring joke/criticism: the blog post itself appears AI-generated (it even ships with an "AI-generated summary" box), which some read as evidence NVIDIA isn't prioritizing the project enough to have a person write the announcement.
  • Practical concerns centered on cuda-oxide's pre-1.0, nightly-only status, meaning bugs could originate from Rust, cuda-oxide, CUDA, or Tile, adding a debugging layer.
  • Pushing back on "LLMs won't know this new tool," one commenter said even a non-frontier model (DeepSeek) competently ported C++ CUDA kernels to cuda-oxide Rust and matched performance.

3 Privacy Tools

Hister: A private search engine for the pages you visit and the files you keep

641 points · 172 comments · github.com

Hister is a self-hosted, open-source (AGPLv3) personal search engine that indexes the full text of web pages you visit, via a Firefox/Chrome extension, and local files you choose to keep, letting you search that index from a web UI, terminal, or an AI assistant connected over MCP. It emphasizes privacy (no telemetry, no mandatory cloud sync, data stays on a server you control), with optional semantic/embeddings-based search, multi-user support, and importers for browser history and bookmarking tools.

What the comments said

  • Commenters noted this is a repeat HN topic and that the project reportedly needs to rename itself after receiving a letter from the similarly named commercial product histre.com.
  • Multiple requested features, like Linkwarden bookmark import, turned out to already be documented and supported, which several people pointed out directly.
  • A strong nostalgia thread recalled that Google Chrome once offered full-text offline search of browsing history (circa 2008, removed by 2013) and that Google Desktop Search offered similar functionality years earlier, with disagreement over whether it was cut for technical reasons or because offline pages don't serve ads.
  • Some pushed back on "just ask ChatGPT to find what I read before" as a solution, arguing it trades one pain point for deeper dependence on a third party, whereas Hister keeps the index local and accessible to any AI assistant via MCP.
  • A few commenters shared their own smaller, more primitive personal history-search tools, welcoming Hister as a more polished alternative.

266 points · 374 comments · gowers.wordpress.com

Mathematician Timothy Gowers explains, in a post cross-posted with Terence Tao, why he did not sign an open letter from 25 Fields medallists warning that AI risks reducing mathematics to mass-produced "true/false" statements at the expense of conceptual understanding. Gowers agrees the field faces a real crisis but argues mathematicians fall along a spectrum between valuing problem-solving and valuing conceptual understanding, so he offers a more nuanced take rather than opposing the letter outright, while worrying publicly taking a side could deepen a rift in the community. He also discloses his early access to OpenAI models and notes that his own automated-theorem-proving research group's founding premise, understanding how humans prove theorems, has been undercut by LLMs succeeding without that understanding.

What the comments said

  • Debate centered on the letter's core claim: whether producing true/false answers via AI risks trapping mathematical progress in a "local maximum" versus whether truth-seeking should simply be the priority regardless of method.
  • A significant current of cynicism toward AI companies held that firms are treating curated, community-built unsolved problems as just another raw resource to extract and monetize, indifferent to the field's long-term health.
  • Others rebutted that fear, arguing math problems ultimately derive from real-world needs, so problems will never run out no matter how capable AI becomes.
  • Several commenters found Gowers' actual stated reasons (not wanting to alienate mathematicians motivated by things other than understanding, and not wanting to prolong drama over inevitable AI adoption) unconvincing or overly diplomatic, accusing him of drawing "a distinction without a difference."
  • Some speculated about Gowers' broader trajectory on AI risk, comparing him to other academics who have grown increasingly alarmed about AGI.

6 Engineering

Wax motor

442 points · 75 comments · en.wikipedia.org

The Wikipedia article explains wax motors, linear actuators that convert heat into mechanical force by exploiting the 5-20% volume expansion of wax as it melts. It details their design (a sealed wax chamber, a plunger, a heat source, and a biasing spring or weight to reset the plunger) and their surprisingly wide range of real-world uses, including aerospace fuel and hydraulic controls, HVAC mixing valves, washing-machine door locks, heating-system zone valves, dishwasher detergent dispensers, and passive greenhouse vent actuators.

What the comments said

  • Several commenters pointed out the article omits the most familiar everyday example, the automotive engine thermostat, which relies on the same wax-expansion principle and is covered under a separate Wikipedia page.
  • Many expressed appreciation for the mechanism's elegant, purely passive simplicity, likening its "analog black box" cleverness to steampunk machinery, with tangents into other old-school control systems like pneumatic logic still running in mechanical rooms decades later.
  • Commenters shared teardown and demonstration videos, including a Big Clive teardown and a greenhouse-vent timelapse, as recommended first-hand viewing, praising one channel for plain, non-clickbait titles.
  • One anecdote recalled a consulting-firm interview brainteaser that hinged on wax actuators, which the commenter hadn't heard of at the time and didn't get the job.
  • General enthusiasm centered on the greenhouse-vent use case, where the motor opens vents purely from ambient heat with no external power needed.

Bend – A language that blocks AI mistakes via proof, on CPU and GPU

504 points · 238 comments · bend-lang.com

Bend 2.0 is a compiled, Python-syntax language that runs in parallel on both CPU and GPU (claimed near-C speed on one core, up to ~100x faster across many), paired with a fast built-in proof checker (based on an affine dependent type theory called BendTT) so AI coding agents can formally verify generated code against user-declared invariants. Developers write "laws" in a LAWS.bend file, and Bend mathematically blocks any AI-generated change that would violate one — demonstrated via a game where an AI-added feature that broke a "you can't win" invariant was rejected until the AI produced code that provably preserved it.

What the comments said

  • Notable backlash over the 2.0 release squashing the git history down to a single commit (erasing contributions from 44 contributors) right after the project's messaging leaned on AI-driven trust; the maintainer (Victor Taelin) acknowledged the commit history contains a lot of "AI slop."
  • A recurring debate on whether formal verification can realistically scale to large, messy real-world codebases ("how do you formally verify Facebook?"), with counterexamples cited — AWS Nitro, Apple's post-quantum CoreCrypto, and Microsoft's Rust verification work — as evidence formal methods are gaining real traction, alongside a note that LLMs increasingly lean on Lean-style proof systems for math.
  • Some commenters raised a "who verifies the verifier" concern: since the laws themselves could be AI-generated and wrong, formal proof just relocates rather than eliminates the trust problem, though one reply suggested laws are meant to stay simple and human-authored while the agent is only responsible for the proof.
  • Technically minded commenters connected the project to Taelin's earlier interaction-combinator/HVM research and asked how it compares to Ada/SPARK, another proof-oriented systems language.
  • Several questioned where the actual compiler lives in the repo, pointing to comp.ts and bend.ts, with the maintainer confirming those files are rough and partly AI-generated for now.

Keys Not Included: recovering the signing keys for US driver's license barcodes

280 points · 148 comments · ryan.science

The author recovers the ECDSA public keys used to sign the barcodes on driver's licenses from New York, Virginia, and North Carolina, exploiting the fact that multiple signatures sharing a key can be used to reconstruct that public key, after reverse-engineering the undocumented signed-message format (the signature field itself must be zeroed before signing). The piece contrasts this with California, whose DMV already publishes its verification keys openly, and argues that since the underlying signing infrastructure clearly already exists at Canadian Bank Note (which issues IDs for five states), the only thing missing is a willingness to publish keys so barcodes can be verified nationwide.

What the comments said

  • A dominant correction thread pushed back on the submission's subtitle: commenters stressed the author recovered only public keys, not private signing keys, since ECDSA math doesn't allow deriving a private key from signatures this way — and noted the article itself is explicit about this.
  • A tangent asked whether similar signed barcodes could stop currency counterfeiting; others countered that static signed data can simply be copied, so it wouldn't help without a challenge-response scheme like NFC passports use.
  • Several argued the deeper flaw is that barcodes don't cryptographically bind to the cardholder's photo, so a valid signature plus a swapped photo would still pass most checks, and pointed to European/passport-style NFC chips (which embed a signed photo) as an already-solved alternative.
  • Personal anecdotes described using a scannable fake ID to pass convenience-store checks with little scrutiny, and one commenter said they deliberately damage their real ID's barcode so store scanners can't harvest their data.
16 AI & ML

HarnessTax: How Much Does the Harness Matter for Coding Agents?

221 points · 92 comments · harnesstax.github.io

The project tests 21 model-harness combinations — seven models (including Claude, GPT, and Kimi variants) run through Claude Code, Codex CLI, and the minimal open-source harness "Pi" — on SWE-bench Lite and Terminal-Bench 2.0. It finds harness choice barely moves success rates but dramatically changes cost: the same model can cost roughly twice as much inside Claude Code as inside Pi, largely because Claude Code's starting context is over 10x larger. It also finds that models often perform best outside their own vendor's "native" harness (9 of 12 model/benchmark pairings), undercutting the assumption that provider-tuned harnesses are optimal.

What the comments said

  • A recurring disagreement was whether calling the overhead a "tax" is fair: some argued Claude Code and Codex's bulk buys real sandboxing/alignment guardrails that Pi lacks, while others countered that sandboxing can be bolted onto Pi cheaply and that most of Claude Code's bloat is verbose tool descriptions, not safety logic.
  • Several pushed back that "harness" is being conflated with "agent," arguing a single-harness, single-model comparison is shallow next to bigger levers like parallel execution, sub-agent delegation, and multi-model routing.
  • Practitioners shared firsthand benchmarking experience showing results vary heavily by inference provider/middleware, not just harness — e.g., one model was reliable via one provider but had frequent tool-calling failures via another, depending on the harness used.
  • A tangent criticized Claude Code's CLI for freezing on terminal resize compared to Codex CLI/OpenCode, alongside broader skepticism about AI company valuations.
  • Multiple commenters said their own experience matches the paper's headline finding — a harness is necessary, but differences between capable ones are overstated — with some preferring thin, minimal wrappers for controllability.

18 AI & ML

Infinite-Parameter LLMs: Generating and Adapting Weights from Live Data

149 points · 40 comments · arxiv.org

This paper proposes the "Infinite-Parameter LLM," an MoE-inspired architecture where a compact hypernetwork generates low-rank weight modulations of a shared base network directly from live interaction data (facts and corrections a user supplies at runtime), rather than storing that information only in the prompt. Unlike prior weight-generators that read context once and freeze, the authors maintain an online-updated Bayesian belief over the generator's latent code, so the effective weights keep being re-derived as a session progresses. The stored parameter footprint stays fixed while the compiled weights are effectively unlimited; the paper proposes an evaluation protocol comparing this to in-context learning and retrieval but does not report results.

What the comments said

  • Excitement about continuous/online learning was tempered by security and alignment worries, including fears of subtle manipulation (an orchestrator biasing outputs toward a product) and models drifting into unwanted "attractor states" like greed or power-seeking without frozen weights as an anchor.
  • Some pushed back on the paper's framing of "scaling laws" as fixed laws rather than an architectural choice, linking to papers questioning the paradigm, while others noted accuracy gains from scale still compound meaningfully even near the ceiling.
  • Multiple commenters were skeptical about how continuously-updating models would remain stable, drawing comparisons to catastrophic forgetting and speculating about vendor lock-in around persistent, stateful model hosting.
  • A clarifying subthread noted the model isn't literally infinite in stored parameters — a fixed-size generator can be recompiled into effectively unlimited weight configurations over time.
  • A speculative tangent imagined a shared system where individual discoveries get folded into a model dynamically, with others raising concerns about data poisoning of such a system.
19 Formal Verification

Developing provably correct Rust code with Verus

159 points · 75 comments · amazon.science

This Amazon Science post by Bryan Parno introduces Verus, an open-source automated program verifier for Rust that checks annotated code against formal mathematical specifications for all possible inputs, going beyond what testing or Rust's type system alone can guarantee. Developers write pre- and post-conditions in Rust-like syntax and get feedback in under a second, and Verus can even verify "unsafe" and concurrent code with custom locking. Amazon uses it to prove correctness of critical infrastructure components like the Nitro Isolation Engine, and it has been adopted by open-source projects such as certificate-validation libraries, data-format parsers, and Kubernetes controllers.

What the comments said

  • Several commenters explain the mechanics: Verus translates proof obligations into SMT problems solved by Z3, the same general approach used by Dafny, Frama-C, and SPARK/Ada — with one noting the article is "more of an advertisement" for AWS's work than a description of anything unique to Verus.
  • A comparison to TLA+ recurs: some argue Verus is more practical because it verifies the actual implementation directly, whereas TLA+ models a design separately and leaves a trust gap to the real C/Rust code.
  • A skeptical thread questions the whole premise — if a spec can itself be wrong or is far more complex than the code, what has really been proven? Others push back that verifying against agreed properties like confidentiality and integrity (as AWS did for the Nitro hypervisor) is still valuable, arguing "don't let perfect be the enemy of good."
  • Commenters point to related tools, notably Aeneas, a Lean-based Rust verifier from Microsoft.
  • Overall reaction leans enthusiastic ("this seems like a big deal"), tempered by the specification-trust debate and accusations of self-promotion.
