France to ban unsolicited telemarketing calls

1,022 points · 485 comments · lemonde.fr

France's new law, pushed by a joint campaign from 11 consumer advocacy groups, bans unsolicited commercial telemarketing (cold calls to consumers) starting August 11, 2026. It replaces the previous opt-out registry system, Bloctel, which critics say was routinely ignored by scammers since compliance was effectively voluntary. Businesses can still contact existing customers about related offers, but new cold outreach to consumers now generally requires prior opt-in consent.

What the comments said

  • Commenters distinguished this law from fraud calls: it targets legitimate telemarketers, while spoofed-number scam calls were already curbed by a separate January 2026 rule forcing telcos to flag unauthenticated calls as "private number."
  • A sub-thread parsed the B2B exceptions: businesses may still call existing customers about upgrades or related products, but cold B2B/B2C outreach now needs opt-in consent, with some arguing calls to personal devices should carry criminal penalties.
  • Some pushed back on the law as "infantilizing," questioning where it stops (street marketing, door-to-door sales), while others noted door-to-door sales are already banned in some countries.
  • One commenter flagged that Bloctel, the prior do-not-call registry, had reportedly been hacked/breached.
  • Several people said invoking GDPR (asking to speak to a "GDPR officer" or revoking consent) already works informally in France to stop unwanted calls.

532 points · 378 comments · bbc.com

4 AI Security

Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs

613 points · 276 comments · stolen-thoughts.com

Researchers show that the encrypted "reasoning" (chain-of-thought) blocks returned by proprietary APIs from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google can be decoded by replaying them into a different, more permissive sibling model and prompting it to recite the hidden content back in plaintext. Applying this to 6,708 real agent trajectories scraped from GitHub and Hugging Face, they reconstructed 315,320 reasoning blocks and recovered 704 distinct privacy artifacts, including 62 API keys, 33 passwords, 24 access tokens, and 30 personal email addresses, 64 of which appeared only inside the hidden reasoning and nowhere in the visible session output. The findings suggest encrypted CoT is less isolated than assumed, since providers decrypt it server-side into whatever model context replays the session.

What the comments said

  • Multiple commenters explained the mechanism: the provider decrypts the reasoning block server-side and injects it into the target model's context, so a weaker sibling model can simply be asked to recite it back in plaintext.
  • The blog's author appeared in the thread confirming he'd attempted a similar cross-model replay (GPT-5.5 into 5.5-mini) but couldn't get the smaller model to leak plaintext, calling this team's success "a good consolation prize" for validating the attack channel.
  • General consensus that this specific exploit is easy for vendors to patch (e.g., binding reasoning blocks to a session/model), but commenters flagged a related, harder-to-defend "Trace Inversion" technique that reconstructs plausible-looking reasoning without needing the real encrypted trace.
  • Some technical curiosity about why replay across models even works, framed as an accidental byproduct of providers wanting reasoning to persist across a conversation when models are swapped mid-session.
  • A few complaints about the site's scroll behavior on mobile amid otherwise positive reactions to the research.

What's the best programming language for coding agents?

251 points · 182 comments · danluu.com

Dan Luu interrogates the widely cited claim that dynamic, terse languages (like Clojure or J) are more "token efficient" for LLMs than static languages, and thus cheaper/better for coding agents. Running his own evals (implementing a zstd decoder from the RFC across languages, at both medium and ultra effort with GPT-5.6), he finds the dynamic-language advantage mostly evaporates at higher effort, with some static languages performing best; his pre-registered predictions that the "weird language supremacy" (J) and the general dynamic-vs-static claim would not hold were largely confirmed.

What the comments said

  • A top request was for the evals to normalize by "equivalent accomplishment" rather than raw token counts, since a boilerplate-heavy language might just reflect what has the best signal-to-noise in training data rather than what LLMs reason best with.
