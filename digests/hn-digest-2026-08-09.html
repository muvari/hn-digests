1 Self-Hosting

My server is a phone now

504 points · 241 comments · seg6.space

Facing rising DRAM prices and unimpressive shared-VPS performance for a browser-automation workload, the author repurposed an old CMF Phone 1 (8-core ARM, 8GB RAM) as a personal server instead of buying new hardware. After a failed attempt to flash postmarketOS bricked the phone's usability, they kept stock Android and used Termux as a lightweight host, running services under runit, tuning Android's battery/idle settings to behave like a server, and using proot-distro to run standard Linux ARM64 OCI images without root or a custom kernel. Tailscale provides a stable address so the phone remains reachable across networks and reboots.

  • Commenters shared related ideas and experiences repurposing old phones as compute nodes, including clustering multiple phones, using them for rendering/streaming, and warnings that sustained (non-bursty) workloads tend to burn phone hardware out quickly.
  • Some discussion focused on Surf, the anti-detect/AI-agent browser tool the setup was built around, with mixed reactions ranging from curiosity to distaste for its bot-browsing use case.
  • Overall reception was positive and appreciative of the detailed writeup, with some readers saying they now plan to repurpose their own old phones.

4 Databases

We replaced Redis with MySQL for inventory reservations and it scaled

333 points · 247 comments · shopify.engineering

Shopify describes migrating checkout inventory reservations off Redis and onto the same MySQL database as their inventory ledger, eliminating a class of overselling/underselling bugs caused by reservations and the ledger living in separate systems that couldn't be updated atomically. Their design uses one row per sellable unit combined with MySQL 8's SKIP LOCKED to avoid lock contention, capping each item/location pool at 1,000 rows (refilled by an inline replenishment process with a lock to prevent thundering-herd re-inserts) to keep queries fast even for items with tens of thousands of units. The approach reportedly held up under Black Friday 2025 peak traffic of $5.1M in sales per minute.

What the comments said

  • A significant contingent of commenters criticized Shopify's leadership (founder and COO) for political views/funding, a tangent largely unrelated to the technical content and pushed back on by other commenters.
  • Several readers were skeptical that the post itself was AI-generated (citing stylistic tells and phrases like the load-bearing-sounding "the real bottleneck wasn't what we were observing and measuring"), which colored some readers' trust in the technical narrative.
  • Technical commenters generally found the SKIP LOCKED / one-row-per-unit design sound and educational, appreciating the explanation of MySQL features, though some raised concerns about the 1,000-row bounded pool as a potential synchronization risk during extreme bursts, countered by others noting it's a standard denormalization-for-performance tradeoff.
  • A broader debate emerged about consolidating on fewer datastores (MySQL only) versus using specialized tools like Redis, with opinions split between valuing operational simplicity and arguing that added complexity should always be justified by necessity.
  • One commenter shared a well-received tangential link to a deep-dive on InnoDB locking, which others found more substantive than parts of the original article.

Dithered QR Codes

364 points · 43 comments · andrewt.net

The author explains how to hide a low-resolution photo inside a QR code by shrinking the data modules and using error-diffusion (Floyd-Steinberg-style) dithering rather than simple thresholding. A key trick is running a first dithering pass specifically to "mask" the fixed data-module pixels, diffusing their large color errors into neighboring pixels so the underlying salt-and-pepper noise becomes far less visible while the code stays scannable. The piece also covers practical caveats: shrinking margins, choosing which modules can be safely altered thanks to QR error correction, and the tradeoff between aesthetics and real-world scan reliability on things like crumpled flyers.

Improving Heuristics for A* Pathfinding

334 points · 37 comments · redblobgames.com

This interactive Red Blob Games article explains how to speed up A* pathfinding by improving its heuristic using precomputed "landmark" nodes rather than just tuning the priority queue or graph size. It walks through the triangle-inequality-based "differential heuristic": by precomputing exact distances from a landmark, you can derive a tighter lower-bound estimate than plain Euclidean/Manhattan distance, and combining multiple landmarks (taking the max of their bounds) covers more start/goal pairs across a map. The article notes landmark placement is project-specific, depending on which paths in a game (e.g., a colony sim) are most common or most performance-critical.

What the comments said

  • Commenters widely praised Red Blob Games as a top-tier resource, with several saying they instantly click on anything from the site.
  • The author's own comment revealing he first learned this technique in 2007 and took until 2026 (after many abandoned attempts since 2015) to write it up clearly drew admiration for the persistence.
  • A minor bug/confusion was flagged and fixed live in an interactive counter that appeared to show the same number before and after, because it's a live-updating value tied to user interaction rather than a static before/after pair.
  • One commenter shared a tangential anecdote about accidentally reinventing "drunken pathfinding" by adding random weight noise to node costs, causing wandering movement.
  • No real disagreement with the article's content; discussion was overwhelmingly positive and technical asides rather than pushback.

Cool URIs Don't Change (1998)

236 points · 59 comments · w3.org

In this 1998 W3C essay, Tim Berners-Lee argues that URIs break not because of any technical necessity but because of organizational carelessness: site reorganizations, file moves, changing implementation mechanisms (like exposing "cgi-bin" in a URL), and lack of forethought about metadata. He rebuts the idea that URNs will solve persistence for us, insisting there's nothing about HTTP that makes URIs inherently unstable, and that with a proper URI-to-document mapping database organizations can keep links stable indefinitely; he cites an NSF publication URL as a good example of a durable, well-structured URI.

What the comments said

  • Multiple people note the article predates modern mitigations like 301/302 redirects and CMS-default slug redirects, arguing SEO incentives have since made URL stability a business priority even though the underlying advice is still rarely followed.

There Are Magic Hexagons of Every Order

188 points · 31 comments · gukov.dev

The author explores magic hexagons, which are provably impossible in "normal" (starting-at-1) form for any order beyond the single known order-3 case, and investigates "abnormal" hexagons with consecutive-but-not-starting-at-1 numbering, where prior best-known results only reached order 9. By restricting to antisymmetric, zero-sum hexagons and discovering that every such hexagon decomposes uniquely into a basis of local 6-cell alternating rings (a recursive "potential field" representation), the author reduces the search space enough to find magic hexagons of every order, with an AI-assisted proof process described in the piece's latter section.

What the comments said

  • Readers largely praised the article for its clarity, interactivity (clickable/hoverable diagrams), and the elegance of the "potential field" abstraction, calling it a highlight beyond a typical math puzzle writeup.
  • One notable critique: the section describing the LLM-assisted proof process was seen as distracting compared to the strong mathematical narrative, with a suggestion the author could have used "we" to keep focus on the math.
  • A side discussion compared magic hexagons' treatment of all lines to magic squares' arbitrary exclusion of short diagonals, with the author and others digging into why pandiagonal squares differ.
  • Commenters raised, and the author confirmed, an edge case: order-2 magic hexagons are impossible even under the relaxed constraints, so the title's "every order" should technically exclude order 2.

116 points · 18 comments · github.com
