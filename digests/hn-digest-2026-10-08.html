1 AI & ML

Whistle: Speech to Text in 16.9 MB

829 points · 168 comments · cactuscompute.com

Cactus released Whistle, a 16.9 MB open speech-to-text model that runs with no dependencies on the same CPU engine as their “Needle” text model, so one binary can transcribe audio and then act on it via tool calls. It transcribes seven languages, auto-detects the language, emits word-level timestamps, and claims an 11 ms time-to-first-token with higher decode throughput than Whisper base and Moonshine tiny, while being far smaller (16.9 MB vs. 145 MB for Whisper base).

What the comments said

  • Many commenters tested the live demo and were impressed by English accuracy, though some reported it struggling with Spanish and heavy accents.
  • Several said raw model size isn't the real bottleneck for them — the genuinely hard problem is transcribing impaired speech, which needs a dictation-tuned model rather than a general STT model.
  • A tangent wished for a spell-checker as good and as unobtrusive, an unrelated gripe aimed at Apple's autocorrect.
  • A lighthearted thread on misreading “Whistle” as literal whistle-to-text, with references to whistled languages like Silbo Gomero.
  • General praise for the benchmark numbers given how tiny the model is.

13 Science & Physics

Teams in Vienna and Beijing have built the first thorium nuclear clocks

184 points · 53 comments · nytimes.com

The article could not be fetched (paywalled/blocked); this summary is drawn from the HN discussion and the linked Nature papers. Two independent teams, in Vienna and Beijing, built the first working nuclear clocks using thorium-229 nuclei (rather than electrons, as in standard atomic clocks) embedded in doped fluorite crystals. Nuclear transitions are far less sensitive to environmental electromagnetic noise than electron-based atomic clocks, and such clocks are predicted to be far more sensitive than current atomic clocks to physics beyond the Standard Model, including possible dark-matter signatures. The Beijing team currently has better signal-to-noise via a different UV frequency-multiplication method; the Vienna team plans to improve theirs.

What the comments said

  • Multiple commenters supplied direct links to both underlying Nature papers and paywall-free mirrors, since the original article didn't link to the primary research.
  • Clarifying discussion on how clock accuracy and drift are actually measured: by comparison against existing optical/atomic clocks, rather than some absolute reference.
  • Several explained why nuclear clocks are considered promising: locking onto the nucleus, which is shielded by electrons, should be far less perturbed by environmental drift than today's complex ion-trap or optical-lattice atomic clocks.
  • A tangent on thorium's natural abundance and role in background radiation led to corrections distinguishing common thorium-232 from thorium-229, an 8,000-year half-life isotope that doesn't occur naturally and must be manufactured.
  • General astonishment at the physics and engineering needed to synchronize and verify clocks at this level of precision.

  • Technical discussion about the PCM3168A codec choice — some called it dated with worse SNR than newer TI parts, though the creator defended it as proven and good value, citing distrust of recent TI op-amp quality issues.
  • A minor joke/concern about a hardcoded default destination IP baked into the firmware.
  • Questions about extending sample rates beyond 96 kHz were answered: the DAC could go to 192 kHz but the ADC on the current codec tops out at 96 kHz.

A Terminal Protocol for Program Status (OSC 7501)

168 points · 52 comments · mitchellh.com
