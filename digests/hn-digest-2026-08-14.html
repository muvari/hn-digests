6 Security & Cryptography

Google is making private AI practical with homomorphic encryption

365 points · 215 comments · blog.google

Google announced HEIR, an open-source compiler that converts pre-trained AI models into ones that run inference directly on fully homomorphically encrypted (FHE) data, so a server can compute results (recommendations, fraud detection, network intrusion detection, hotword detection) without ever seeing the underlying plaintext. Google frames this as shifting the privacy/capability trade-off from an impossibility to a cost problem, and highlights partnerships with hardware-accelerator makers and academic collaborators to push down FHE's traditionally steep computational overhead.

What the comments said

  • Heavy skepticism about practicality: commenters cite roughly 1000x computational overhead for FHE inference, and one links a paper showing single-CPU-thread inference latencies of 24 to nearly 500 seconds, arguing this remains commercially unviable beyond toy models.
  • A technical debate over what security guarantees FHE actually provides — some clarify it cryptographically satisfies IND-CPA (ciphertexts reveal nothing about plaintext) but does not by itself prove the server ran the agreed-upon computation, only that it can't see the data.
  • General distrust of Google persists regardless of cryptographic guarantees, with some arguing 'if it's on someone else's server it isn't yours,' countered by others explaining that's precisely what FHE is designed to neutralize.
  • Side discussion recommending an older blog post on 'programmable cryptography' as useful background reading on FHE and related techniques.

16 AI & ML

Don't classify, hallucinate

231 points · 96 comments · softwaredoug.com

The post proposes an alternative to structured-output LLM classification for constrained taxonomies (e.g., classifying e-commerce search queries into hundreds of category paths): rather than shipping the full legal vocabulary to the model, ask a cheap/small LLM to 'hallucinate' a plausible-sounding but fictional classification, then resolve that hallucinated label to the nearest real category using embedding similarity search. The author demonstrates this on the Wayfair WANDS dataset, arguing it's much cheaper at scale than sending large taxonomy schemas on every request while still producing usable results.

What the comments said

  • A commenter asks whether this was validated with A/B testing; the author replies it performs a bit worse than shipping the vocabulary to a larger model but is a major improvement over not classifying at all.
  • One commenter shares a firsthand anecdote of an earlier, pre-structured-outputs project that sent a full 40k-token taxonomy per request — calling it expensive and still error-prone.
  • Some pushback on reliability: a commenter warns that embedding nearest-match alone doesn't hold up under rigorous testing and that accuracy-sensitive use cases need a heavier verification/reranking step on top of the embedding shortlist.
  • Alternative approaches are proposed, such as retrieving top-N candidate categories via vector search and having the LLM just output a single digit to pick among them to cut output token costs; a side debate weighs LLM call latency against traditional search speed.
