RISC-V: They Should Have Known Better

291 points · 352 comments · dmitry.gr

Dmitry.GR argues RISC-V will win the cheap-microcontroller market on price alone, not because it's well designed. His core complaints: making nearly everything optional (multiplication, division, interrupts, privilege modes) fragments implementations into incompatible camps; the instruction encoding scatters immediate bits inconsistently and compressed instructions use nine conflicting encoding schemes; obviously useful instructions like bit-test-and-branch are missing; and interrupt latency is markedly worse than ARM's Cortex-M0. He attributes this to designers building from scratch instead of improving the existing OpenRISC ISA.

What the comments said

  • Many commenters broadly agreed, several recounting their own early excitement about RISC-V curdling into disillusionment once real silicon shipped with the ISA's "facepalming special cases"
  • A detailed technical sub-thread covered extension detection: since there's no central authority preventing vendor custom-extension bits from colliding, building a reliable cross-vendor "supported features" bitmask is difficult
  • A specific factual dispute arose over ARM's TBZ/TBNZ instruction, with the article's author clarifying ARM has a separate CBZ/CBNZ for boolean branches, so bit-branching serves a genuinely different purpose
  • A broader RISC-vs-CISC tangent argued the real story is that out-of-order execution won, not RISC-ness per se, with some defending x86 as less bloated in practice than older CISC designs
  • A few floated a community-driven "RISC-6" fork as a fix, but others countered that an ISA can't be truly community-owned without a community-owned fab

The mathematical beauty of hyperbezier curves

194 points · 40 comments · linebender.org

Raph Levien proposes "hyperbezier" curves — defined by a Cesàro equation expressing curvature as a function of arc length — as a candidate replacement for cubic Béziers in 2D vector graphics. The family closely approximates cubic Béziers at small angles but diverges usefully at larger ones, exactly containing analytic curves like the Euler spiral, circular arcs, and log-aesthetic curves that Béziers can only approximate. He demonstrates it can reach sharp squircle-like corners Béziers can't, and highlights its relevance to the classic Kac isospectral-drum problem.

What the comments said

  • Commenters playing with the interactive demo found hyperbezier control points don't feel obviously more intuitive than cubic Béziers — the curve can "snap" abruptly between configurations or saturate so further control-point movement has no effect
  • A key disagreement: one commenter argued Bézier's real strength was "local support" (predictable, localized response to moving a control point), which hyperbeziers may sacrifice for better curvature quality
  • Some noted hyperbeziers can't form loops or cusps the way cubic Béziers can, though most agreed that's rarely needed for typeface or vector-graphic design
  • A commenter asked why the post didn't compare against B-splines or Catmull-Rom splines; another explained those are mathematically convertible to/equivalent with Bézier curves
  • Several nostalgic tangents surfaced, including a claim that Quake 3 Arena was many people's first exposure to Bézier curves

A spectre is haunting Unicode

214 points · 74 comments · dampfkraft.com

The piece traces Japan's 1978 JIS X 0208 encoding standard's "ghost characters" (幽霊文字) — several kanji with no known meaning, pronunciation, or clear historical source. A 1997 investigation found most originated from cataloging mistakes, such as a character accidentally created when two paper cutouts were pasted together and the seam was mistaken for a stroke during copying; one character has no traceable origin at all beyond a likely misreading of another kanji. Because these errors were codified into JIS and later into Unicode via CJK unification, the invented characters are now permanently embedded in essentially every computer's character set.

What the comments said

  • Commenters praised the author (Paul McCann/polm) as a respected figure in Japanese NLP tooling, citing his fugashi tokenizer wrapper and past work on spaCy
  • Discussion connected the JIS ghost characters to the much larger phenomenon in the Kangxi dictionary and to Unicode's CJK Han unification, with debate over whether unification was a pragmatic blunder or a deeper philosophical disagreement
  • On the article's one fully unexplained character, a commenter cited Japanese sources suggesting it actually traces to a faded/misread newspaper scan, refining the piece's hypothesis into something closer to confirmed
  • Lighter cultural notes: fan fiction treats the ghost characters as names of individual yōkai, and someone pointed out the hammer-and-sickle symbol (☭) is its own quirky Unicode inclusion
  • General consensus was that despite being erroneous inventions, the characters are now permanently locked into decades of encoding infrastructure

Abdominal fat predicts heart disease risk better than BMI

245 points · 174 comments · acc.org

A JACC-published study from the Cross-Cohort Collaboration followed over 260,000 people for an average of 20 years and found that waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) reclassify cardiovascular risk beyond what BMI alone predicts. People with "normal" BMI but elevated central adiposity had 15-50% greater risk across most of nine studied cardiovascular outcomes, while people with obese BMI but low WC/WHR showed no significantly elevated risk versus normal-weight, low-WC peers. The authors argue clinicians should incorporate waist-based measures into routine cardiovascular risk assessment rather than relying solely on BMI.

What the comments said

  • Many commenters said this isn't new information — that BMI's blindness to fat distribution and the value of waist measurements have long been discussed clinically — while others found it useful as large-scale confirmation
  • Extended debate on why BMI persists despite known flaws: it requires no special equipment and is "good enough" as a population-level filter, even though it misclassifies individuals like muscular athletes
  • Practical sub-thread on how to actually assess visceral fat, covering waist-measurement protocol uncertainties versus DEXA scans as a more precise but costlier alternative
  • A tangent argued ECG- and imaging-based risk models are more accurate than anthropometric measures, prompting some cynical remarks about testing incentives in cardiology
  • General agreement that fat distribution (visceral vs. subcutaneous) matters more than total mass, with anecdotes about people who appear overweight from subcutaneous fat alone but show no elevated risk
